\noindent \textbf{ABSTRACT:} How does external demand shape recovery from economic downturns in emerging and developing economies? Using monthly activity indicators to identify 263 downturn episodes across 34 economies, we construct a shift-share measure of foreign demand that links predetermined bilateral export shares to trading partners' subsequent growth. Recoveries are faster and output losses smaller when established export markets in advanced economies expand during the downturn. Aggregate trade openness, by contrast, shows no comparable association with either downturn depth or recovery duration. In our baseline specification, this relationship is driven primarily by the United States: conditional on downturn depth and an active IMF arrangement, a one-percentage-point increase in the US demand contribution---the pre-downturn US export share times US real GDP growth---is associated with a six-month reduction in the duration of a full negative cycle. Alternative recovery definitions confirm the role of advanced-economy demand, though the concentration of the estimate in the US market depends on the required persistence of recovery. We interpret these patterns through a core-periphery model in which core expansions of equal size generate different international spillovers depending on their import content. External stabilization thus depends not on aggregate openness or headline partner growth alone, but on whether expanding partners generate import demand for a country's exports.

\section{Introduction} \blfootnote{\emph{Disclosure of AI use.} An AI large language model was used to develop the code for the paper's data pipeline and figures and to assist with language and grammar editing. The author is responsible for the analysis, results, conclusions, and any remaining errors.}
Sri Lanka's recent crisis and recovery illustrate the complementarity between domestic stabilization and external demand. Following its first sovereign default in 2022, real GDP contracted by more than 7 percent. An IMF-supported arrangement approved in March 2023 provided balance-of-payments financing, anchored fiscal consolidation, and supported debt restructuring. By 2024, output growth had rebounded to roughly 5 percent as domestic stability took hold and external inflows resumed: tourist arrivals rose by 38 percent to 2.1 million, tourism receipts increased by about two-thirds, and workers' remittances recovered as the exchange rate stabilized. These inflows returned the current account to surplus and rebuilt international reserves. While the Fund-supported program established the domestic conditions for stabilization, external demand for Sri Lanka's services and labor supplied the proximate momentum for recovery.

This paper examines whether this pattern holds more broadly across emerging and developing economies: while domestic policy adjustment provides the foundation for stabilization, external demand governs how quickly activity recovers. Our focus is on the geographic structure and composition of foreign demand. Two economies with identical ratios of trade to GDP can experience very different recovery paths depending on which markets absorb their exports and whether those destinations continue to grow during the downturn.

Using monthly activity indicators across 34 emerging and developing economies, we document a strong empirical association between recovery speed and growth in established export destinations, particularly advanced economies and the United States. In our baseline specification, demand from the United States serves as the primary external anchor. Conventional measures of aggregate trade openness---such as total exports plus imports relative to GDP---show no statistically significant relationship with either downturn depth or recovery duration. Because aggregate openness measures gross cross-border volume irrespective of partner performance, it is uninformative about whether a country's specific buyers are expanding. Recovery depends instead on effective demand along predetermined bilateral export links.

Cross-border transmission also depends on the composition of growth within partner economies. Historically, US expansions have generated sizeable spillovers abroad because US expenditure has a high propensity to import goods and services. Future expansions in major economies may transmit more weakly, however, if growth shifts toward sectors with lower import intensity---such as domestically provided artificial intelligence services, reshored manufacturing, or domestic defense procurement. Under such a shift, headline GDP in the core economy can expand while generating substantially less import demand for trading partners.

Recent changes in the global trading system underscore this distinction between export demand and aggregate trade. Despite widespread discussion of geoeconomic fragmentation, world trade volumes and dollar values reached record highs in 2025, and the global trade-to-GDP ratio has remained near its historical peak. What has changed is the bilateral direction of flows. China has become the largest trading partner for a majority of economies, even as the United States has pared back its direct bilateral trade with China. As documented in Section~\ref{sec:facts}, however, China's expanding trade footprint with emerging markets has occurred almost entirely on the import side. For most economies in our sample, export shares to China remain small. This asymmetry is central to business-cycle stabilization: foreign demand aids recovery by absorbing a country's exports, whereas rising import penetration alters input sourcing and relative prices without directly lifting domestic output during a slump.

\paragraph{Contribution.} This paper studies how partner-specific external demand shapes recovery across 263 downturn episodes dated from monthly activity indicators. Building on earlier work linking trading-partner growth to recovery from currency crises \citep{deb2006}, we use a shift-share design that interacts pre-downturn bilateral export shares with destination growth over the episode window. Decomposing foreign demand across the United States, other advanced economies, emerging markets, and China uncovers pronounced heterogeneity across partners. In baseline duration regressions, demand from the United States accounts for most of the recovery association, whereas exposure to China behaves differently, reflecting its role primarily as an upstream supplier and commodity buyer rather than a broad market for emerging-market exports.

Using pre-downturn export weights isolates predetermined trade links from post-crisis trade adjustment. Because the baseline partner-growth window overlaps with the recovery period, we treat the main estimates as conditional associations and probe timing and reverse causality through contraction-phase regressions, crisis-year partner growth, and state-dependent monthly local projections. We further implement a leave-one-out import-demand design that excludes each exporting country's own shipments from destination import growth and applies exposure-robust inference \citep{adao2019, borusyak2022}. Finally, we formalize the mechanism in a two-region core-periphery model that treats export exposure as risk diversification and shows how a compositional shift toward import-light growth in the core weakens external stabilization.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:lit} places the analysis in the literature. Section~\ref{sec:facts} documents the reorganization of global bilateral trade and the asymmetry of China's trade expansion. Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents the static risk-sharing framework and its dynamic extension. Section~\ref{sec:empirics} describes the data, episode dating, and empirical design. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the main estimates, regional-hub decompositions, local projections, and robustness exercises. Section~\ref{sec:scenario} discusses the implications of an import-light shift in core growth, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:lit}

Our analysis connects several literatures on crisis recovery, international business-cycle transmission, and the structure of global trade.

The first is the empirical literature on output recoveries following sudden stops and balance-of-payments crises. \citet{calvo2006} document ``Phoenix miracles,'' in which output in emerging markets rebounds from systemic sudden stops with little recovery in domestic bank credit, pointing to recovery margins outside the domestic financial sector. We examine demand from established export markets as one such margin and show that, in our sample, this channel operates largely through trade links to the United States. In a duration study of currency crises, \citet{deb2006} finds that trading-partner growth shortens recovery times. We extend this approach to a broader set of downturns dated from monthly activity series and disaggregate foreign demand across the United States, other advanced economies, emerging markets, and China. In doing so, our results complement work on the global financial cycle \citep{rey2013}: alongside financial conditions transmitted by US monetary policy, US real import demand serves as a distinct trade channel shaping peripheral recoveries.

Our findings also speak to the debate on whether trade openness amplifies or dampens macroeconomic volatility. Whereas \citet{rodrik1998} and \citet{digiovanni2009} emphasize that openness exposes economies to external shocks and sector-specific risk, \citet{eichengreen2024} find that trade integration bolsters macroeconomic resilience. Our results help reconcile these views. Aggregate trade openness by itself is orthogonal to both the depth of downturns and the speed of recovery in our panel; what matters is the identity and cyclical performance of trading partners. Openness toward a stable, expanding buyer accelerates recovery, whereas an equally open economy exporting to volatile or stagnant markets receives little cyclical support.

A related literature studies international business-cycle comovement and shock propagation \citep{kose2003, ciccarelli2010}. Focusing on downturn episodes allows us to ask which bilateral links actively pull economies back toward trend. This asymmetry also bears on macroeconomic forecasting. \citet{sholomytskyi2026} documents that IMF World Economic Outlook projections capture only part of the empirical spillover from US growth to emerging markets, leaving forecast errors correlated with US activity. Our results offer a trade-based explanation: US growth transmits to exposed economies through direct demand for their exports, whereas Chinese growth links to many of the same economies primarily through import supply. Forecasting frameworks that treat headline growth across major hubs as symmetric sources of external demand will therefore misstate cross-border spillovers.

The analysis also informs assessments of macroeconomic adjustment under IMF-supported programs. An evaluation by the Independent Evaluation Office finds that Fund arrangements support growth best when domestic adjustment is complemented by benign external conditions \citep{ieo2021}. Our shift-share measures quantify those external demand conditions directly. Where established export markets are expanding, external demand reinforces adjustment; where trading partners stagnate or during synchronized global contractions, official financing must substitute for a missing trade engine.

Methodologically, we draw on the shift-share literature \citep{adao2019, goldsmith2020, borusyak2022} to construct predetermined bilateral exposure weights and exposure-robust standard errors. Substantively, these bilateral weights allow us to contribute to the literature on geoeconomic fragmentation \citep{aiyar2023, gopinath2024, fv2024}. Rather than summarizing fragmentation through an aggregate factor that can conflate trade policy with the business cycle, we separate export destinations from import origins. Over the period studied, aggregate world trade did not contract; instead, import sourcing shifted toward China while export reliance on advanced economies remained intact. Distinguishing export markets from import suppliers is essential because only the former provide demand stabilization during domestic contractions.

\section{The geography of connectedness}\label{sec:facts}

To document recent shifts in global trade patterns, we use monthly bilateral merchandise trade flows from the IMF's International Trade in Goods Statistics (IMTS). These data also provide the pre-downturn export shares used in the shift-share demand measure in Section~\ref{sec:empirics} and the aggregate trade indices across income groups. Throughout the paper, bilateral trade shares are computed over trailing twelve-month windows to remove seasonal fluctuations and high-frequency noise.

Despite ongoing debate over geoeconomic fragmentation, aggregate world trade expanded steadily over the late sample period. World merchandise trade volumes grew by $2.8$ percent in 2024 and $2.4$ percent in 2025; by the third quarter of 2025, merchandise trade stood $4.5$ percent higher year-on-year, reaching a record level in nominal US dollars. Commercial services trade expanded even faster, rising by $6.8$ percent in 2024 and $4.6$ percent in 2025 \citep{wto2026}. Merchandise trade growth carried into early 2026 even as services growth moderated \citep{unctad2026}, and the global trade-to-GDP ratio remained close to its historical peak.

A notable share of this expansion was concentrated in technology hardware. Capital goods linked to artificial intelligence---including advanced semiconductors, servers, and telecommunications equipment---accounted for roughly one-seventh of world merchandise trade in 2025 and generated about two-fifths of total trade growth \citep{wto2026}. Because production of these inputs is concentrated in a small number of manufacturing hubs, aggregate trade growth has coincided with rising product and geographic concentration at the technological frontier.

The main structural shift lies in the bilateral direction of trade. By 2023, China was the largest bilateral trading partner for 60 economies, compared with 33 for the United States, and roughly 70 percent of countries traded more in gross terms with China than with the United States. Yet this shift has been markedly asymmetric across imports and exports. Between 2021 and early 2026, China's share of national imports rose in 74 percent of countries, with a median gain of about two percentage points, whereas national export shares to China remained essentially flat. Most economies increased their reliance on Chinese inputs and final goods without a corresponding expansion in sales to the Chinese market.

The United States moved in the opposite direction, substantially reducing its direct bilateral trade with China. Between 2021 and early 2026, China's share of US goods imports fell by more than half to roughly 8 percent, and China's share of US exports declined by a similar proportion. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:facts}, the Chinese share of US imports fell below the rest-of-world median around 2023. While most economies deepened their import links with China, the United States decoupled directly from Chinese supply.

This asymmetry between import sourcing and export destinations has direct macroeconomic implications. Expanding Chinese manufacturing supply has lowered import prices and reshaped input sourcing across emerging markets, but China's domestic market absorbs only a small fraction of the exports on which these economies rely when domestic demand contracts. Across the 34 emerging and developing economies in our estimation sample, the median merchandise export share going to China rose only modestly over two decades---from $0.8$ percent in 2005 to $1.6$ percent in 2024 among non-commodity exporters, and from $2.2$ percent to $7.8$ percent among commodity exporters---whereas the median combined export share going to the United States and the European Union stood at $50.0$ percent in 2024. For the countries studied here, China has grown primarily as an upstream supplier rather than as a source of final export demand.

Annual US goods imports rose to roughly \$3.3 trillion even as direct imports from China fell to half their 2022 peak; imports from Vietnam and Mexico expanded sharply, with their combined shipments to the United States now more than double those of China. Aggregate trade thus continued to grow as bilateral trade networks reconfigured. The remainder of the paper examines how these bilateral links shape recovery from downturns.

\section{A model of trade as risk sharing}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{The risk-sharing mechanism}

Consider a small open economy $i$ whose output, in log deviations from trend, is a weighted average of domestic demand and export sales to a large core economy:
\begin{equation}
y_i = (1-s_i)\,\varepsilon_i + s_i\,\mu ,
\label{eq:portfolio}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_i$ is an idiosyncratic domestic shock with variance $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, $\mu$ is core demand with variance $\sigma_\mu^2$, $\operatorname{cov}(\varepsilon_i,\mu)=0$, and $s_i\in[0,1]$ is the share of economy $i$'s output sold to the core.\footnote{In Equation~\eqref{eq:portfolio}, $s_i$ denotes exports to the core as a share of total output ($X_{i,\text{core}}/Y_i$). In the empirical specifications of Section~\ref{sec:empirics}, we decompose partner demand using bilateral shares of total exports ($X_{ij}/X_i$, which isolate directional partner composition) while controlling separately for the aggregate trade-to-GDP ratio.} Equation~\eqref{eq:portfolio} admits a standard portfolio interpretation: total output places weight $1-s_i$ on volatile domestic demand and weight $s_i$ on external demand, yielding variance $\operatorname{Var}(y_i)=(1-s_i)^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2 + s_i^2\sigma_\mu^2$. This formulation follows the classic view of trade as international risk sharing \citep{obstfeld1994, kalemliozcan2003} and links to the broader literature on trade structure and volatility \citep{rodrik1998, digiovanni2009}. We augment this setup by allowing the import intensity of core expenditure to vary through a composition parameter $\theta$, building on literature linking demand composition to trade elasticities \citep{bussiere2013}.

Output variance $\operatorname{Var}(y_i)=(1-s_i)^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2+s_i^2\sigma_\mu^2$ is strictly convex in the export share $s_i$ and attains a unique minimum at
\begin{equation}
s^\star=\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2+\sigma_\mu^2},
\end{equation}
since $\partial \operatorname{Var}(y_i)/\partial s_i = 2\,[\,s_i(\sigma_\varepsilon^2+\sigma_\mu^2)-\sigma_\varepsilon^2\,]$ is negative for $s_i < s^\star$ and positive for $s_i > s^\star$. When domestic and foreign shocks are orthogonal, a marginal increase in export exposure from autarky ($s_i=0$) lowers output variance for any finite $\sigma_\mu^2$, as $\partial \operatorname{Var}(y_i)/\partial s_i|_{s_i=0} = -2\sigma_\varepsilon^2 < 0$. When the core economy is more stable than the periphery ($\sigma_\mu < \sigma_\varepsilon$), the variance-minimizing export share exceeds one-half.

As in standard portfolio choice, selling to a large, uncorrelated market stabilizes income because foreign demand persists when domestic demand contracts. If domestic and foreign shocks covary, the marginal effect at autarky becomes $2(\operatorname{cov}(\varepsilon_i,\mu)-\sigma_\varepsilon^2)$. Trade exposure reduces output variance whenever $\operatorname{cov}(\varepsilon_i,\mu) < \sigma_\varepsilon^2$, but this diversification benefit weakens as business-cycle synchronization rises and disappears during a common global contraction. Partner stability also reflects macroeconomic policy: a core economy with effective countercyclical policy maintains a lower $\sigma_\mu$, indirectly stabilizing its trading partners.

Now suppose core expenditure comprises two components: import-intensive spending (such as consumer goods and tradable equipment), which generates demand for peripheral exports, and import-light spending (such as domestic services, nontraded structures, or domestically sourced capital), which does not. Let $\theta \in [0,1]$ denote the share of core absorption allocated to import-light activity. Peripheral economy $i$'s effective export exposure is then $s_i(\theta) = \omega_i(1-\theta)$, where $\omega_i$ is its baseline structural trade link. The parameter $\theta$ captures the composition of core expenditure: a shift toward domestically provided artificial intelligence services, reshored production, or domestic defense procurement raises $\theta$ and lowers the import content of a given level of core GDP.

\begin{corollary}\label{prop:tilt}
Suppose $\omega_i > 0$ and the effective export share lies in the sub-optimal range $s_i(\theta) = \omega_i(1-\theta) < s^\star$. As the core economy shifts expenditure toward import-light activity ($\theta$ rises), output variance in the periphery strictly increases:
\[
\frac{\partial\operatorname{Var}(y_i)}{\partial\theta} = -\omega_i\,\frac{\partial\operatorname{Var}(y_i)}{\partial s_i} > 0.
\]
Headline core GDP can continue to grow as $\theta$ rises---since import-light production contributes to domestic output---even as the stabilizing impulse transmitted to trading partners declines.
\end{corollary}

For an illustrative parameterization with $\sigma_\varepsilon=5$ and $\sigma_\mu=2$, trade integration reduces domestic output variance by up to $86$ percent at $s^\star \approx 0.86$. Increasing the import-light share $\theta$ from $0.2$ to $0.5$ raises periphery output variance by roughly $16$ percent on average across the three baseline exposure regimes ($\omega_i \in \{0.10, 0.30, 0.55\}$), with the largest increases occurring at high baseline exposure $\omega_i$ (Figure~\ref{fig:layer1}). A compositional shift toward import-light growth in the core thus erodes external risk sharing without any slowdown in headline core GDP.

\paragraph{Assessing partner quality.} The model implies that a trading partner serves as an effective cyclical buffer when its demand grows steadily, exhibits low volatility ($\sigma_\mu$), and covaries weakly with the exporter's domestic shocks. Crucially, the relevant variable is the partner's \emph{import demand} rather than its headline GDP, since foreign output stabilizes the periphery only insofar as it translates into purchases of peripheral exports.

Table~\ref{tab:partnerquality} compares the three major economic hubs along these dimensions. Evaluated by import demand, the United States ranks highest: real import growth of roughly 4 percent per year combined with low volatility yields a mean-to-standard-deviation ratio of $0.62$. The European Union displays low volatility but slower import growth ($0.30$). China exhibits the fastest average import growth but also the highest volatility, yielding an intermediate ratio of $0.43$. Ranking hubs by the mean-to-volatility ratio of headline GDP growth instead (last column of Table~\ref{tab:partnerquality}) would place China first, conflating domestic production growth with cross-border demand pull. Section~\ref{sec:results} tests whether this distinction matters for post-crisis recovery.

\subsection{A dynamic illustration}
To trace how these compositional differences play out over the course of a downturn, we embed the mechanism in a simple dynamic two-region gap model. The model is designed to isolate a single channel: two expansions of equal size in the core economy generate different recovery paths in the periphery when their import intensity differs.

Core activity consists of import-intensive absorption, $\hat c_t$, and import-light domestic activity, $\hat q_t$. A reduced-form stabilization rule, $\hat r_t=\phi_y\hat y_t^C$, moderates core fluctuations without driving the cross-border comparison. Core output and imports are given by
\begin{align}
\hat y_t^C &= \omega_c\hat c_t+\omega_q\hat q_t, \\
\hat m_t &= \eta_c\hat c_t+\eta_q\hat q_t, \qquad \eta_c>\eta_q\geq0.
\end{align}
While both sectors contribute to core output, import-intensive absorption has higher import content ($\eta_c > \eta_q$). An expansion in the import-light sector raises core imports by less per unit of output and can even depress aggregate imports if resource competition crowds out import-intensive absorption.

A peripheral economy $i$ receives core import demand in proportion to its predetermined bilateral export exposure $\gamma_i$:
\begin{align}
\hat x_{i,t} &= \gamma_i\hat m_t,\\
\hat y_{i,t}^P &= \rho_P\hat y_{i,t-1}^P+\alpha\hat x_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}^P.
\end{align}
Following an adverse domestic shock $\varepsilon_{i,t}^P < 0$, external demand supports recovery through export revenue, and recovery time is defined as the first period in which peripheral output returns to trend.

We simulate an import-intensive expansion and an import-light expansion scaled to produce the same unit impact increase in core output ($\Delta \hat y_0^C = 1$). Because the import-intensive shock raises core imports substantially more, it pulls exposed peripheral economies back to trend more quickly, with larger gains where bilateral exposure $\gamma_i$ is high. An import-light expansion provides much weaker external support. Figure~\ref{fig:layer2} plots the calibrated impulse responses; Appendix~\ref{app:model} presents the full system, formal proposition, and calibration.

\section{Data and empirical strategy}\label{sec:empirics}

\paragraph{Sample selection.} Our empirical analysis focuses on emerging and developing economies that have experienced macroeconomic or external distress. Our sampling frame begins with the 97 countries (93 emerging and developing economies and 4 advanced economies) that had an IMF-supported arrangement approved between 2005 and 2025 in the Monitoring of Fund Arrangements (MONA) database. Selecting countries that accessed Fund resources over this two-decade window provides a well-defined pool of economies subject to balance-of-payments pressures, while MONA's arrangement dates allow us to control directly for whether an IMF program was active during each downturn.

Because annual GDP data are too coarse to measure recovery durations accurately and quarterly national accounts are unavailable or short for many developing economies, we track business cycles at the monthly frequency. We compile monthly central-bank economic activity indices (prioritized where available) and industrial production series from Haver Analytics for the 57 MONA emerging and developing economies with monthly series coverage, seasonally adjusting raw series via X-13ARIMA-SEATS.

Across these 57 countries, 34 have complete bilateral trade and macroeconomic control data and experience at least one qualifying downturn episode between 2004 and 2024 (the remaining 59 of the 93 MONA emerging and developing economies form the excluded MONA comparison group in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:samplecomp}). As shown in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:samplecomp}, the 34 included countries are similar to excluded MONA countries and to non-program emerging markets in average GDP growth, trade openness, and output volatility, though they are somewhat larger and include a higher share of commodity exporters, which we control for directly in the regressions.

\paragraph{Episode dating and duration metrics.} We identify downturn episodes from the Hodrick--Prescott (HP) output gap of each country's monthly log activity index ($100 \times \ln \text{Index}$, smoothed with monthly parameter $\lambda = 129{,}600$; in real-time one-sided robustness checks, the expanding filter is initialized after a 36-month burn-in window). An episode is defined as a contiguous period of negative output gaps whose trough reaches at least 2 percent below trend. Across the 34 countries over 2004--2024, this rule yields 263 episodes, providing substantially more sample variation than annual crisis chronologies.

Downturn depth is measured as the absolute value of the minimum output gap during the episode (in percent). We examine three duration outcomes (in months): \emph{contraction} (from the initial negative-gap month $t_{\text{peak}}$ to the trough month $t_{\text{trough}}$, which equals zero if the trough occurs in the initial month of the spell), \emph{climb} (from $t_{\text{trough}}$ until the first month $t_{\text{rec}}$ in which the output gap returns to zero or above), and the \emph{full negative cycle} ($t_{\text{rec}} - t_{\text{peak}}$). For the eight spells that do not return to trend by the end of the sample, both climb and full-cycle durations are independently right-censored at 60 months. Recovery thus corresponds to output returning to its trend path rather than surpassing its pre-downturn peak level.

\paragraph{External demand measure.} We measure foreign demand using a shift-share construction. For country $i$ in episode $e$ with peak calendar year $t(e)$, external demand is the export-weighted average of trading partners' annualized real GDP growth over the three-year window $[t(e),\, t(e)+2]$, weighted by predetermined bilateral export shares:
\begin{equation}
\text{External Demand}_{ie} = \sum_{j} s_{ij, e}^{\text{pre}} \cdot \bar{g}_{j, e},
\label{eq:ext_demand}
\end{equation}
where $s_{ij, e}^{\text{pre}}$ is country $i$'s average bilateral merchandise export share to partner $j$ over the three calendar years preceding the peak year ($[t(e)-3,\, t(e)-1]$), normalized across reporting country partners so that $\sum_j s_{ij,e}^{\text{pre}} = 1$, and $\bar{g}_{j,e}$ is partner $j$'s mean annual real GDP growth rate over $[t(e),\, t(e)+2]$. We decompose $\text{External Demand}_{ie}$ into four mutually exclusive partner groups $k$: the United States, other advanced economies, emerging markets excluding China, and China. Merchandise export shares come from the IMF's IMTS database; in robustness exercises we supplement goods trade with World Bank data on commercial services exports, international tourism receipts, and workers' remittances.

\paragraph{Empirical specification.} We regress downturn depth and recovery durations for country $i$ in episode $e$ on the partner-demand terms, pre-downturn trade openness (merchandise exports plus imports divided by GDP in the peak year), and episode controls $\mathbf{X}_{ie}$ (downturn depth in the duration regressions, partner growth volatility over the episode window in the depth regression, and an active IMF-arrangement indicator), clustering standard errors by country:
\begin{equation}
\text{Duration}_{ie} = \sum_{k} \beta_k \text{Demand}_{ie}^k + \gamma_1 \text{Openness}_{ie} + \mathbf{X}_{ie}'\boldsymbol{\delta} + \varepsilon_{ie}.
\label{eq:main_spec}
\end{equation}
Every regression includes an indicator equal to one if an IMF-supported arrangement was approved within one year of the episode peak year, so that partner-demand coefficients are estimated conditional on official program participation (capturing program presence rather than financing scale or conditionality).

\paragraph{Identification and empirical interpretation.} Fixing bilateral export shares over the three years prior to each episode ensures that endogenous post-crisis trade diversion does not contaminate the exposure weights. Because partner GDP growth is averaged over $[t(e),\, t(e)+2]$ and overlaps with the recovery phase, however, common global or regional shocks could affect both partner growth and domestic recovery. We therefore interpret the baseline OLS estimates as conditional associations rather than isolated structural causal effects.

To assess sensitivity to common shocks and timing, we disaggregate demand across partner hubs, control for non-US world growth, regional growth, and peak-year fixed effects, and re-estimate the relationship using peak-year partner growth, peak-to-trough contraction duration, and monthly state-dependent local projections. Because the US growth shift takes 21 distinct peak-year values across 34 countries (yielding roughly 22 effective shock-level clusters), we report country-clustered standard errors alongside wild cluster bootstrap $p$-values and two-way clustering by country and peak year.

To address reverse causality and common shocks more formally, we construct a leave-one-out destination import-demand instrument. For country $i$ in episode $e$, the leave-one-out shift is
\begin{equation}
Z_{ie}=\sum_j s_{ij,e}^{\text{pre}}\,g_{j,e}^{-i},
\label{eq:loo_shift}
\end{equation}
where $s_{ij,e}^{\text{pre}}$ is the pre-downturn export share to destination $j$ over $[t(e)-3,\, t(e)-1]$ and $g_{j,e}^{-i}$ is the average annual growth rate over $[t(e),\, t(e)+2]$ of destination $j$'s aggregate imports from all reporting origins other than country $i$. Netting out country $i$'s own shipments removes mechanical reverse causality from a country's export rebound to measured partner import growth. We also residualize the annual destination import growth rates via two-way additive demeaning with respect to destination and year fixed effects ($\alpha_j + \tau_t$) and compute exposure-robust standard errors at the destination-shock level following \citet{adao2019} and \citet{borusyak2022}. Under this design, identification requires that residualized destination import shocks be orthogonal to unobserved determinants of country $i$'s recovery conditional on controls, without requiring the trade shares themselves to be exogenous.

While the leave-one-out design rules out direct mechanical feedback, annual bilateral trade data lack product-level disaggregation, the three-year import window still overlaps part of the recovery, and destination growth may simultaneously transmit through remittances, tourism, commodity prices, or financial conditions. We therefore view the leave-one-out estimates as quasi-experimental corroboration of the direction and partner breakdown of our baseline findings rather than an isolated structural goods-trade elasticity. Full estimates, balance tests, and diagnostic checks are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:robust}.
\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

The baseline estimates show a strong association between trading-partner growth and recovery speed, driven primarily by demand from the United States.

Table~\ref{tab:main} decomposes external demand by partner group across downturn depth and the three duration measures. Foreign demand from advanced economies is associated with a statistically significant reduction in both the climb from trough to trend and the full negative cycle. Disaggregating advanced economies further shows that this relationship stems largely from the United States: the coefficient on other advanced economies is smaller and significant only at the 10 percent level on the full cycle (Table~\ref{tab:main}).\footnote{A Wald test rejects equality of the US and other-advanced coefficients on the full negative cycle at the 10 percent level (difference of $3.1$ months, $t=-1.9$, $p=0.07$), indicating that the US coefficient is larger in magnitude rather than merely more precisely estimated.}

The implied economic magnitude is sizeable. A one-percentage-point increase in the US demand contribution is associated with an estimated six-month reduction in the full negative cycle.\footnote{In our estimation sample, the interquartile range of the US demand contribution is $0.8$ percentage points, implying that an interquartile shift corresponds to a cycle roughly $4.7$ months shorter (a standardized effect of $0.25$ standard deviations).} Because the demand contribution is the product of the pre-downturn US export share $s_{i,\text{US},e}^{\text{pre}}$ and annualized US real GDP growth $\bar{g}_{\text{US},e}$ over the three-year window, a one-percentage-point increase represents a substantial demand impulse: for an economy sending one-third of its exports to the United States, it corresponds to an additional 3 percentage points of annualized US real GDP growth over the window (or 10 percentage points for an economy with a 10 percent US export share).

Demand from other emerging markets enters with a small, statistically insignificant coefficient. By contrast, the China term enters with a positive and statistically significant coefficient for downturn depth, the climb, and the full cycle, though not for the contraction phase. Higher pre-downturn export exposure to China is thus associated with deeper contractions and longer downturns. As shown below, the depth association is driven by commodity exporters and reflects the global commodity cycle rather than direct bilateral trade transmission. For the median economy in our sample, China accounts for less than $0.5$ percent of exports---too small a share for Chinese domestic demand to provide meaningful export pull. This export-side pattern mirrors the import-sourcing asymmetry documented in Section~\ref{sec:facts}.

Because the baseline partner-growth window spans the peak year and the two subsequent years, part of the window overlaps with the recovery itself. Re-estimating a single-partner specification using only peak-year US GDP growth sharply reduces this overlap and leaves the US coefficient strongly negative ($-3.8$, $t=-4.1$, compared with $-5.3$, $t=-3.5$ in the corresponding three-year single-partner specification; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prewindow}). Contraction-phase regressions and monthly local projections below point to the same conclusion.

\input{../output/tables.tex}

Unlike partner-specific demand, aggregate trade openness shows no statistically significant relationship with either downturn depth or recovery duration: the trade-to-GDP ratio is insignificant across all columns of Table~\ref{tab:main}. Because gross trade openness treats all trading partners alike, it does not distinguish between economies selling into expanding markets and those tied to stagnant ones. Cyclical stabilization depends on growth in established export destinations rather than overall trade volume.

Trading-partner volatility---the export-share-weighted standard deviation of partners' annual GDP growth over the window---enters the depth regression with a positive and significant coefficient. Economies linked to more volatile partners suffer deeper troughs, consistent with external demand fluctuations transmitting through bilateral trade links. Recovery prospects are strongest for economies linked to large, stable, and growing markets, and weakest for those tied to volatile partners and distant from major demand hubs.

Because macroeconomic stability in partner countries partly reflects countercyclical policy, we also construct a predetermined measure of partner stabilization capacity: the export-weighted average of each partner's historical rebound strength (its GDP growth in years immediately following domestic contractions), computed strictly from WEO data from 1980 up to each episode's peak year. On its own, predetermined partner stabilization capacity predicts shallower troughs and shorter downturns; once realized partner demand is added, however, the predetermined proxy is absorbed while the US demand term remains significant (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:stabilization}). Consistent with the model, a partner's stabilization capacity matters because it sustains effective import demand during downturns.

Finally, we test whether the positive coefficient on China exposure across downturn depth and recovery duration reflects commodity-market exposure. Interacting the China demand term with a commodity-exporter indicator across all four outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:china}) shows that the positive associations with downturn depth ($2.68$, $t=7.4$), the climb phase ($3.53$, $t=3.8$), and the full negative cycle ($3.36$, $t=2.7$) are driven entirely by commodity exporters. For non-commodity exporters, the China interaction term is negative and statistically indistinguishable from zero across every phase ($-4.06$, $t=-1.1$ on depth; $-5.56$, $t=-1.4$ on the full cycle). Furthermore, controlling for commodity-exporter status strengthens both the US demand coefficient ($-7.07$, $t=-5.3$ on the full cycle) and the non-US advanced-economy coefficient ($-2.88$, $t=-2.2$).

\subsection{Recovery through regional hubs}

Dropping any single country changes the US full-cycle coefficient only slightly, confirming that no individual country drives the estimate; however, removing the six most US-exposed economies together reduces the coefficient to zero.\footnote{The leave-one-country-out US full-cycle coefficient ranges from $-5.2$ to $-6.6$. Because episode counts vary across countries---from 29 in Costa Rica to one in several economies---we also re-estimate the model weighting each country inversely by its episode count (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}); this yields a US full-cycle coefficient of $-8.4$ ($t=-4.5$) and a non-US advanced-economy coefficient of $-4.5$ ($t=-2.7$), indicating that high-frequency repeat episodes attenuate rather than drive the baseline estimates.} This pattern is an inherent feature of shift-share designs \citep{goldsmith2020}, which identify the effect from cross-sectional units with nontrivial exposure to the hub.

To distinguish genuine bilateral exposure from coincidental timing with the US business cycle, we conduct a permutation test on the single-partner US specification. Holding each episode's realized US growth fixed, we randomly reassign the cross-sectional vector of pre-downturn US export shares across countries 2{,}000 times. The actual full-cycle coefficient ($-5.3$) is more negative than every one of the 2{,}000 placebo draws ($p<0.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:placebo}), confirming that the estimate relies on the bilateral structure of US export links rather than common time variation.

To examine regional structure directly, we assign each economy to its primary export hub---the United States, the European Union, or China---based on its modal pre-downturn export share, and estimate hub-specific demand regressions (Table~\ref{tab:hubs}).

The sample partitions into three groups (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appcountries}): nine economies export primarily to the United States (103 episodes across Mexico, Central American and Caribbean economies, Colombia, Ecuador, and Jordan), twenty-four export primarily to the European Union (156 episodes across emerging European, African, and Central/South Asian economies alongside South American commodity exporters such as Argentina, Chile, Peru, Paraguay, and Suriname), and one economy, Mongolia (4 episodes), exports primarily to China.\footnote{Assigning hubs by each country's modal pre-downturn export share partitions the 34 economies cleanly across the three blocs. Because Chile's episode peaked in 2008 and Peru's episodes peaked between 2004 and 2013---before China overtook the European Union as their top export destination---both fall into the European group during their sample episodes.}

\input{../output/tables_hubs.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:hubs} reports the regional hub estimates. In pooled regressions that include hub-bloc fixed effects to absorb between-bloc differences in average duration (columns 1--2), own-hub demand significantly shortens both the post-trough climb ($-3.38$, $t=-3.3$, wild bootstrap $p=0.006$) and the full negative cycle ($-3.34$, $t=-2.8$, wild bootstrap $p=0.021$), whereas non-hub partner demand is smaller and statistically insignificant ($-1.29$, $t=-1.6$).\footnote{Omitting hub-bloc fixed effects in the pooled regression attenuates the own-hub coefficient ($-1.40$, $t=-0.9$) because Mongolia---the sole China-hub economy---experienced a prolonged 60-month commodity slump alongside high average Chinese GDP growth, creating a positive between-bloc correlation.} Estimating the relationship separately within blocs (columns 3--4) or via hub-bloc interactions in the pooled sample (column 5) shows that recovery covaries strongly with US demand among US-linked economies ($-4.52$, $t=-3.1$, wild cluster bootstrap $p=0.008$ across the nine clusters; $-4.69$, $t=-4.2$ without non-hub controls) and more moderately with European demand among Europe-linked economies ($-2.57$, $t=-1.8$). The smaller European estimate reflects slower average growth in the European Union over the sample period: to shorten downturns abroad, a trade hub must not only be large, but expanding.

These results show that recovery is tied to growth in the specific hub to which an economy exports, with the United States providing the strongest pull owing to its combination of import absorption and sustained growth. China, by contrast, serves as the primary export destination for only one economy in the sample (Mongolia). Over our sample period, China integrated with most emerging markets as a supplier of imports rather than a primary buyer of exports, explaining why rising gross trade with China has not created an alternative recovery anchor.

This geographic pattern also clarifies which economies face the greatest cyclical vulnerability. Because gravity limits export penetration into distant markets, proximity to a slow-growing hub (as in emerging Europe) provides only modest stabilization, while economies distant from any expanding hub and reliant on volatile commodity markets face the weakest external support during downturns.

\paragraph{Inference and robustness.} With 34 country clusters, asymptotic cluster-robust standard errors could over-reject in finite samples. A restricted wild cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2008} confirms the statistical significance of the US full-cycle coefficient at $p<0.001$. In addition, because multiple countries in a given year experience the same US growth realization, we compute two-way clustered standard errors by country and peak year \citep{cameron2011}. Two-way clustering raises the standard error only slightly ($1.7$ versus $1.6$), leaving the US full-cycle coefficient statistically significant at the 1 percent level ($t=-3.4$, $p=0.003$).

The baseline estimate holds across a wide range of alternative specifications (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). Because downturn depth is determined jointly with recovery and may absorb part of the external-demand effect, omitting the post-onset depth control increases the magnitude of the US full-cycle coefficient to $-6.6$ ($t=-4.1$). Weighting each country inversely by its episode count yields $-8.4$ ($t=-4.5$). Restricting attention to the peak-to-trough contraction phase---which reduces overlap with post-trough partner growth---likewise yields a negative and significant US coefficient ($-2.23$, $t=-3.1$ in the four-partner specification of Table~\ref{tab:main}; $-2.1$, $t=-3.4$ when US demand enters alone).

Results are also robust to alternative detrending methods, minimum-duration rules, and crisis definitions (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}): using a one-sided real-time HP filter ($-7.1$, $t=-3.4$), replacing the HP filter altogether with the \citet{hamilton2018} linear-projection filter ($-5.0$, $t=-3.0$ on US demand and $-3.5$, $t=-2.0$ on other advanced demand), requiring negative-gap spells to last at least three months ($-5.5$, $t=-3.0$ on US demand and $-4.2$, $t=-3.2$ on other advanced demand), bridging isolated one-month non-negative readings between spells ($-5.4$, $t=-2.7$), restricting the sample to the 25 episodes that match systemic crises in the \citet{laeven2020} database ($-22.3$, $t=-2.3$), or varying the trough depth threshold between 1.5 and 3 percent all preserve a negative and statistically significant US coefficient. The China coefficient, by contrast, is fragile: it loses significance under the one-sided HP filter, the Hamilton filter, the minimum-duration and blip-merging rules, and the 3 percent threshold.

To test whether partner demand merely proxies for commodity price movements, we control for country-specific commodity terms-of-trade shocks using the IMF Commodity Net Export Price Index with fixed trade weights \citep{gruss2019}, measured over the same window and interacted with the commodity-exporter indicator (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:commodity}). The commodity terms-of-trade impulse is nearly orthogonal to the US demand contribution in our sample ($r=0.03$). Including it leaves the US coefficient essentially unchanged on both the full cycle ($-5.6$, $t=-3.6$) and the contraction phase ($-2.2$, $t=-3.1$), while the commodity terms themselves are statistically insignificant.

\paragraph{Censored duration models.} Because eight episodes do not return to trend within the 60-month window, right-censoring could bias linear OLS duration estimates. We therefore estimate survival models that account explicitly for censoring. Non-parametric Kaplan--Meier survival curves split at the median US demand contribution show that episodes with above-median US demand pull exit downturns faster across the entire distribution (median recovery of 4 months versus 8 months; log-rank $p<0.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:km}).

Estimating discrete-time proportional hazards models with a complementary log-log link and country-clustered standard errors confirms this result across three specifications (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:survival}). A one-percentage-point increase in the US demand contribution yields a hazard ratio of $2.25$ ($z=4.3$) on the full cycle with baseline controls (column 1), $2.25$ ($z=4.2$) when omitting the post-onset downturn depth control (column 2), and $1.96$ ($z=4.7$) when starting the survival clock at the episode trough to model the post-trough climb phase directly (column 3, where non-US advanced-economy demand also enters with a hazard ratio of $1.22$, $z=1.8$).

\paragraph{Alternative recovery metrics.} Because episode dating, depth, and duration are all derived from the HP-filtered output gap, a brief movement above zero could prematurely record a recovery. We therefore re-estimate the baseline specification using two outcomes that do not rely on a single zero crossing: (i) \emph{persistent-return} duration, which requires the output gap to remain non-negative for at least three or six consecutive months; and (ii) \emph{cumulative output loss}, defined as the integrated negative output gap over the episode (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:altrecovery}).

External demand continues to predict faster recovery under these definitions. In Panel A of Table~\ref{tab:altrecovery}, both aggregate partner demand ($-1.5$, $t=-2.0$) and combined advanced-economy demand ($-2.5$, $t=-2.3$) significantly shorten the three-month persistent-return duration. Disaggregating by partner group in Panel B shows that non-US advanced-economy demand enters at $-3.0$ ($t=-2.2$) and emerging-market demand at $-2.0$ ($t=-1.9$) under the three-month persistence rule, while the US demand contribution strongly reduces cumulative output loss ($-34.0$, $t=-3.7$).

At the same time, the degree to which the duration estimate loads on the United States alone depends on the persistence requirement. Under the three-month persistence rule, the standalone US duration coefficient moderates to $-2.0$ ($t=-1.2$) as the stabilizing association is shared across advanced-economy hubs rather than concentrated solely in the US term (while at the six-month horizon all disaggregated duration coefficients are imprecisely estimated). The baseline six-month estimate should thus be read as the association with initial return to trend rather than a definition-invariant constant. Across metrics, the durable finding is that recoveries are faster when established advanced-economy export markets expand.

\subsection{Counterfactual portfolio reweighting}

To gauge the quantitative scope of trade reorientation, we conduct a simple partial-equilibrium accounting exercise using the full-cycle coefficients from Table~\ref{tab:main} evaluated at each episode's realized partner growth rates.

Shifting five percentage points of a country's pre-downturn export share toward its primary regional hub---drawn proportionally from non-hub partners---shortens the full negative cycle by about half a month for the median US-linked economy (median $-0.5$ months, interquartile range $-0.6$ to $-0.3$; Table~\ref{tab:counterfactual}). By linearity, a ten-percentage-point reallocation toward the US market corresponds to roughly a one-month reduction.

For Europe-linked economies, the same five-percentage-point reallocation toward the European hub yields almost no change in duration (median near zero), because the European Union grew more slowly over the sample period than the emerging-market partners from which the share is drawn. When the five-percentage-point shift toward the regional hub is funded instead by reducing export shares to China, both US- and Europe-linked blocs see a median duration reduction of roughly half a month.

\input{../output/tables_counterfactual.tex}

This calculation underscores two points. First, reorienting exports toward a regional hub shortens downturns only to the extent that the hub itself is growing. Second, because a five-percentage-point shift changes recovery duration by only half a month, the multi-month cross-country differences documented earlier reflect deep, long-standing differences in trade geography---such as sending half of national exports to the United States versus exporting primarily to distant or volatile markets---rather than marginal trade adjustments. These calculations are linear accounting exercises rather than general-equilibrium counterfactuals.

\subsection{Services trade and workers' remittances}

Our baseline measure uses merchandise exports because bilateral partner breakdowns are available across countries and time, yet foreign demand also reaches emerging markets through commercial services, tourism, and workers' remittances. Because comprehensive bilateral series for services and remittances are unavailable for our panel, we use aggregate pre-downturn intensity measures from the World Bank's World Development Indicators to run three robustness checks that retain the full four-partner baseline specification and controls of Table~\ref{tab:main} (Table~\ref{tab:services}).

First, adding pre-downturn ratios of services exports to GDP, remittances to GDP, and tourism receipts to total exports leaves the US goods-demand coefficient strongly negative ($-5.40$, $t=-3.6$) alongside non-US advanced-economy demand ($-2.64$, $t=-1.7$), while the level intensity terms are individually insignificant (Table~\ref{tab:services}, column 1). Second, interacting the US goods-demand contribution with indicators for top-tercile services and remittance dependence (including uninteracted main effects) leaves the baseline US demand slope significant ($-4.85$, $t=-2.1$) and yields statistically insignificant interaction terms ($-2.16$, $t=-0.8$ and $-0.35$, $t=-0.1$; column 2), indicating that the goods-demand channel is not attenuated in services- or remittance-reliant economies. Third, augmenting aggregate goods partner demand with GDP-share-weighted services and remittance exposures to advanced-economy growth yields a negative and statistically significant coefficient on full-cycle duration ($-1.44$, $t=-2.1$; column 3). Merchandise exports thus capture the bilaterally observable component of a broader external-demand channel.

\subsection{The response to a US-demand impulse}

To trace dynamic responses at the monthly frequency, we estimate state-dependent local projections (LP) of log domestic activity on a US demand impulse, defined as twelve-month US industrial production growth multiplied by each country's pre-downturn US export share.\footnote{Regressions include country fixed effects, the lagged output gap, the regime indicator, and two lags of monthly activity growth, with country-clustered standard errors. The impulse is standardized to unit variance.}

To prevent short-lived noise around trend from generating spurious regime switches, regimes are defined over persistent cyclical phases: an economy is classified in recession when its output gap is below $-0.2$ percent inside a continuous negative-gap spell lasting between one and seven years, and in expansion when the gap exceeds $+0.2$ percent. Near-trend observations (between $-0.2$ and $+0.2$ percent) and brief dips under one year are omitted.

A one-standard-deviation US demand impulse raises domestic activity in both regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:lp}, Table~\ref{tab:lp}). In normal times, the cumulative response peaks at about $1.5$ percent after eight to nine months. During recessions, the response builds more gradually, reaching $0.73$ percent at eighteen months and remaining on an upward trajectory.\footnote{The expansion response peaks at $1.5$ percent (SE $0.24$, $t=6.1$), and the recession response reaches $0.73$ percent (SE $0.24$, $t=3.0$) at month eighteen.} Defining regimes simply by the sign of the output gap yields a similar path with a slightly smaller eighteen-month recession response ($0.60$ percent versus $0.73$ percent).

\subsection{Hub growth in local and global downturns}

Whether an export hub stabilizes a trading partner depends on the hub's growth \emph{during the partner's downturn}. A hub that continues to expand while the domestic economy contracts supplies countercyclical demand; a hub that contracts at the same time transmits the slump.

Figure~\ref{fig:chances} illustrates this distinction by plotting mean hub GDP growth during countries' idiosyncratic downturns (excluding the global contractions of 2008--2009 and 2020) alongside hub growth during global crises. Outside global crises, regional hubs expand during nearly all domestic downturns, with a median growth rate of $1.9$ percent. Economies linked to the United States face the most favorable external environment, with US real GDP growing at roughly $2.5$ percent on average during their downturns, compared with $1.0$ to $1.5$ percent for the European Union. Pooling idiosyncratic downturns with global crises obscures this cyclical buffer because synchronized contractions pull down average partner growth.

This comparison highlights a key boundary condition: external demand insures against \emph{idiosyncratic} downturns, but fails during synchronized global recessions. In 2009 and 2020, both major advanced-economy hubs contracted simultaneously---the United States by roughly 2 percent and the European Union by nearly 5 percent---turning trade links from a stabilizing buffer into a conduit for external contraction \citep{rodrik1998}. Although China continued to grow during both episodes, its share of exports from our sample economies (outside Mongolia) was too small to offset the collapse in Western demand. Our empirical findings thus characterize recovery from country-specific or regional downturns rather than global recessions.

This boundary condition helps clarify the respective roles of trade and official balance-of-payments financing. During idiosyncratic downturns in countries linked to an expanding hub, export demand supports output recovery while official financing bridges temporary liquidity shortfalls. During synchronized global downturns---or for economies without access to a growing export hub---the trade buffer is absent, leaving official financing as the principal external cushion. Accounting for a country's bilateral export links thus indicates where trade can be expected to aid recovery and where official financing must carry a larger share of the adjustment burden.

\section{Implications: if the anchor turns import-light}\label{sec:scenario}

Standard trade-diversification arguments suggest that shifting export shares toward faster-growing economies should improve macroeconomic stability by raising export-weighted partner growth. Our results qualify this view: to act as a cyclical stabilizer, a destination must both absorb a meaningful share of a country's exports and maintain import demand when the exporting economy is in recession. For most countries in our sample, China satisfies neither condition: median export shares to China are below $0.5$ percent, and Chinese import demand covaries closely with domestic investment and commodity cycles.

For economies integrated with the United States, US final demand has historically served as the primary external stabilizer. A natural forward-looking question is how transmission would change if US growth became markedly less import-intensive. Several ongoing shifts could lower the marginal import propensity of US expenditure: a transition in artificial intelligence investment from imported hardware toward domestic software and services, manufacturing reshoring, or higher defense spending concentrated in domestic suppliers. In each case, a given expansion in US GDP would generate less foreign import demand than a traditional consumption-led expansion.

As the model in Section~\ref{sec:theory} illustrates, cross-border transmission depends on the import content of core growth rather than headline core GDP alone. A shift toward import-light growth in the core weakens the external pull available to trading partners during downturns even if headline core growth remains robust. Manufacturing and agricultural exporters in the Americas would be most exposed to such a shift, as slower growth in Europe and low export penetration into China limit alternative demand buffers.

This mechanism also has implications for multilateral surveillance and forecasting. As shown by \citet{sholomytskyi2026}, IMF World Economic Outlook projections historically understate growth spillovers from the United States to emerging markets. If future US growth becomes less import-intensive while upstream supply links with China continue to deepen, forecasting models that rely on historical GDP-to-GDP elasticities without tracking import composition risk misjudging external support during recoveries. These scenarios represent conditional implications of our empirical estimates rather than unconditional forecasts.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies how bilateral trade linkages shape recovery from economic downturns across emerging and developing economies. Using monthly activity series to identify 263 downturn episodes across 34 countries, we find that recoveries are significantly faster when an economy's established export destinations continue to grow. This relationship follows regional hub lines, with the United States serving as the primary anchor for the Americas and the European Union playing a smaller parallel role for emerging Europe. In our baseline specification, a one-percentage-point increase in the US demand contribution---the pre-downturn US export share multiplied by US real GDP growth---is associated with a six-month reduction in the full negative cycle.

Aggregate trade openness, by contrast, shows no systematic association with either downturn depth or recovery duration. Because gross trade openness sums across all partners regardless of direction or cyclical performance, it fails to capture whether a country's export markets are actually expanding. Our results also highlight the asymmetric macroeconomic role of China's rise in global trade. Over our sample period, China expanded rapidly as an import supplier to emerging markets while absorbing a very small share of their non-commodity exports. Consequently, rising trade integration with China on the import side has not created a substitute consumer of last resort.

Several caveats apply to these findings. Because downturn episodes, depth, and duration are constructed from the same HP-filtered monthly activity series, mechanical correlation cannot be dismissed a priori. Even so, the core finding holds across one-sided filters, alternative trough thresholds, systemic crisis chronologies, censored survival models, cumulative output loss measures, and a leave-one-out import-demand design that removes direct export feedback. At the same time, while the US demand contribution strongly reduces cumulative output loss and baseline return-to-trend duration, the concentration of the duration effect in the United States alone versus other advanced-economy markets is sensitive to requiring three or six consecutive months above trend before dating recovery.

A central boundary condition is that foreign demand buffers idiosyncratic downturns but cannot insure against synchronized global contractions. When major advanced-economy hubs contract simultaneously---as in 2008--2009 and 2020---trade links transmit the global slump rather than cushioning domestic output. In such episodes, and for countries isolated from expanding demand hubs, official balance-of-payments financing becomes all the more critical precisely because the external trade engine is unavailable.

Finally, our framework underscores that the composition of growth in major economies matters alongside its pace. If future expansions in the United States or other core economies shift toward domestically sourced services, reshored manufacturing, or domestic defense spending, headline GDP growth in the core will generate weaker import demand and smaller recovery spillovers abroad. Monitoring the import intensity and bilateral direction of partner growth is therefore essential for assessing external resilience in emerging and developing economies.

\paragraph{Leave-one-out import-demand instrument.}
Whereas the baseline shift-share measure weights pre-downturn export shares by partner GDP growth, the leave-one-out specification weights the same shares by the annualized growth of each destination's aggregate imports from all suppliers other than the exporting country over the three-year window $[t(e),t(e)+2]$. For episode $e$ in country $i$, equation~\eqref{eq:loo_shift} combines predetermined export shares $s_{ij,e}^{\text{pre}}$ with leave-one-out destination import growth $g_{j,e}^{-i}$, where destination imports are measured as the sum of bilateral exports reported by all other trading partners. Excluding country $i$'s own shipments removes direct mechanical feedback from domestic export recovery to measured foreign demand.

Because annual bilateral trade data are not disaggregated by product across our panel, the leave-one-out shifts cannot be constructed at the destination-by-product level. We residualize annual destination import growth with respect to additive destination and year fixed effects (via two-way demeaning) before averaging over $[t(e),t(e)+2]$ to purge secular destination trends and the common global trade cycle. Identification requires that these residualized destination import shocks be orthogonal to unobserved determinants of country $i$'s recovery conditional on controls.

Table~\ref{tab:loo} reports the results. The leave-one-out import-demand shift has a correlation of $0.47$ with the baseline GDP-based measure. It enters the full-cycle regression with a coefficient of $-0.27$ ($t=-2.6$) under country-clustered standard errors and $-0.91$ ($t=-3.7$) when constructed from destination- and year-residualized import shifts. Disaggregating the leave-one-out shift by partner group reproduces the baseline pattern in underlying regressions: the US component is negative and statistically significant ($-0.88$, $t=-3.0$ on the full cycle), whereas the other-advanced, emerging, and China components are statistically insignificant. Exposure-robust confidence intervals following \citet{adao2019} and \citet{borusyak2022}, computed at the destination-shock level, preserve statistical significance, although asymptotic shock-level inference should be interpreted with caution given the small effective number of major hubs (inverse Herfindahl index of $5.8$ destinations and median largest share of $0.33$).

Three diagnostic exercises evaluate the plausibility of the design. First, regressing peak-to-trough contraction duration on the leave-one-out shift yields a smaller and statistically insignificant coefficient ($-0.06$, $t=-1.5$), indicating that the full-cycle estimate primarily reflects the post-trough climb phase rather than the initial contraction. Second, covariate balance regressions show no significant association with commodity-exporter status and only a marginal association with aggregate trade openness. Third, dropping each country's single largest export destination and re-computing the shift-share variable yields a series correlated at $0.97$ with the baseline instrument and a similar regression coefficient. While these checks mitigate concerns over mechanical reverse causality, observable sorting, and single-partner dominance, they cannot rule out non-trade spillovers from destination growth or temporal overlap within the three-year measurement window.

\section{The dynamic core-periphery model}\label{app:model}

Whereas the static portfolio model in Section~\ref{sec:theory} illustrates how trading with a stable partner lowers output variance, this appendix presents a simple dynamic two-region model to show how the composition of core growth shapes recovery durations in the periphery. The model is deliberately parsimonious: rather than providing a quantitative general-equilibrium model of the US economy, it traces the transmission from core expenditure composition to core imports, bilateral peripheral exports, and peripheral recovery time.

\paragraph{The core economy.}
Core activity comprises two sectors: import-intensive absorption, $\hat c_t$ (such as consumer goods and tradable equipment), and import-light domestic activity, $\hat q_t$ (such as domestically supplied services or import-substituting production). Import-light activity follows a stationary autoregressive process:
\begin{equation}
    \hat q_t = \rho_q \hat q_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t^q,
    \qquad 0<\rho_q<1.
    \label{eq:q_process}
\end{equation}

Import-intensive absorption satisfies a forward-looking Euler equation and can be crowded out by expansion in the import-light sector:
\begin{equation}
    \hat c_t
    = \mathbb{E}_t \hat c_{t+1}
    - \sigma \hat r_t
    - \chi \hat q_t
    + \varepsilon_t^c,
    \qquad \varepsilon_t^c = \rho_c \varepsilon_{t-1}^c + u_t^c,
    \qquad \sigma>0,\quad \chi\geq0,\quad 0<\rho_c<1.
    \label{eq:core_absorption}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\chi \geq 0$ captures factor-market and expenditure competition between sectors, allowing an expansion in import-light activity to crowd out import-intensive absorption without imposing crowding out by assumption.

A reduced-form stabilization rule closes the core block:
\begin{equation}
    \hat r_t = \phi_y \hat y_t^{C},
    \qquad \phi_y\geq0,
    \label{eq:small_rule}
\end{equation}
where $\hat r_t$ represents the real financing rate. The rule dampens aggregate core fluctuations equally across shocks and does not drive the compositional ranking.

Core output and aggregate core imports are linear combinations of the two components:
\begin{equation}
    \hat y_t^{C}
    = \omega_c \hat c_t + \omega_q \hat q_t,
    \qquad \omega_c>0,\quad \omega_q>0,
    \label{eq:core_output_new}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \hat m_t
    = \eta_c \hat c_t + \eta_q \hat q_t,
    \qquad \eta_c>\eta_q\geq0.
    \label{eq:core_imports_new}
\end{equation}
The condition $\eta_c>\eta_q$ is the key assumption: a unit of import-intensive absorption generates more import demand than a unit of import-light activity. Combining equations~\eqref{eq:core_absorption} and \eqref{eq:core_imports_new}, an import-light shock $\varepsilon_t^q$ affects core imports directly through $\eta_q$ and indirectly through $-\eta_c\chi$ via absorption crowding out. Consequently, core imports can rise modestly, remain flat, or decline even as headline core output expands.

\paragraph{Bilateral transmission to the periphery.}
Peripheral economy $i$ captures a share of core import demand proportional to its predetermined bilateral export exposure $\gamma_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat x_{i,t} = \gamma_i \hat m_t,
    \qquad \gamma_i\geq0,
    \label{eq:bilateral_exports}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_i$ is the theoretical counterpart to the pre-downturn bilateral export share in the empirical shift-share specification.

Peripheral output gap dynamics follow
\begin{equation}
    \hat y_{i,t}^{P}
    = \rho_P \hat y_{i,t-1}^{P}
    + \alpha \hat x_{i,t}
    + \varepsilon_{i,t}^{P},
    \qquad 0<\rho_P<1,\quad \alpha>0.
    \label{eq:periphery_output_new}
\end{equation}
An adverse domestic shock $\varepsilon_{i,0}^{P} < 0$ pushes peripheral output below trend, and external demand shapes the subsequent recovery path exclusively through export sales $\hat x_{i,t}$, scaled by $\gamma_i$.

\paragraph{Equal-output experiments.}
To isolate the role of expenditure composition, we scale the import-intensive shock $\varepsilon_0^c$ and the import-light shock $\varepsilon_0^q$ so that both produce the same impact increase in core output:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \hat y_0^{C}\big|_{\varepsilon^c}
    =
    \Delta \hat y_0^{C}\big|_{\varepsilon^q}.
    \label{eq:equal_output_normalization}
\end{equation}
Under this normalization, any difference in peripheral recovery stems solely from the path of core imports. Because $\eta_c>\eta_q$, an import-intensive expansion generates a larger import path.

Define recovery duration for peripheral economy $i$ as the first period in which its output gap returns to zero following the domestic shock:
\begin{equation}
    T_i
    = \min\left\{h\geq0:\hat y_{i,h}^{P}\geq0\right\}.
    \label{eq:recovery_time}
\end{equation}
This duration metric corresponds directly to the empirical full negative cycle.

\begin{proposition}[Composition of core growth and peripheral recovery]
\label{prop:composition_recovery}
Consider two core expansions normalized to produce the same impact increase in core output. For any peripheral economy with $\alpha\gamma_i>0$, if the $\rho_P$-discounted cumulative difference in core imports is non-negative at horizon $t$, $\sum_{j=0}^{t}\rho_P^{t-j}(\hat m_j^{c}-\hat m_j^{q})\geq 0$ (which holds automatically whenever $\hat m_j^{c}\geq \hat m_j^{q}$ for all $j\leq t$), then
\[
    \hat y_{i,t}^{P,c} \geq \hat y_{i,t}^{P,q}.
\]
If this inequality holds strictly for all $t < T_i^{q}$, then
\[
    T_i^{c} \leq T_i^{q}.
\]
Moreover, the gap between the two peripheral output paths is strictly increasing in bilateral exposure $\gamma_i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Subtracting equation~\eqref{eq:periphery_output_new} across the two experiments under identical initial conditions and domestic shocks yields
\[
    \Delta \hat y_{i,t}^{P}
    = \rho_P \Delta \hat y_{i,t-1}^{P}
    + \alpha\gamma_i\Delta \hat m_t,
\]
where $\Delta \hat m_t=\hat m_t^{c}-\hat m_t^{q}$. Iterating backward to $t=0$ gives
\[
    \Delta \hat y_{i,t}^{P}
    = \alpha\gamma_i
      \sum_{j=0}^{t}\rho_P^{t-j}\Delta \hat m_j.
\]
Since $\alpha>0$, $\gamma_i>0$, and $\rho_P\in(0,1)$, a non-negative discounted sum of import differences implies $\Delta \hat y_{i,t}^{P} \geq 0$. A strictly higher trajectory prior to zero crossing cannot reach zero at a later date, implying $T_i^c \leq T_i^q$. Linearity in $\gamma_i$ implies that the difference scales proportionally with bilateral exposure.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The stabilization parameter $\phi_y$ in equation~\eqref{eq:small_rule} scales the amplitude of core fluctuations by leaning against aggregate output, but does not alter the relative import intensity of the two sectors. Setting $\phi_y=0$ removes endogenous interest-rate stabilization; raising $\phi_y$ dampens both expansions while preserving the ranking of core imports whenever $\eta_c > \eta_q$.

Depending on parameter values, the model encompasses three cases following an import-light core expansion: (i) when $\eta_q > 0$ and crowding out $\chi$ is small, core imports rise, but by less than under an equal-sized import-intensive expansion; (ii) when direct import demand $\eta_q$ roughly offsets absorption crowding out, core output expands with little change in imports; and (iii) when crowding out dominates (for instance, under aggressive import substitution), core output rises while core imports contract. Across all three cases, peripheral recovery depends on the import content of core growth and bilateral export exposure $\gamma_i$.

\paragraph{Calibration and simulation.}

\section{Data and code}\label{app:code}
Bilateral merchandise trade data are drawn from the IMF's International Trade in Goods Statistics (IMTS), real and nominal GDP series from the IMF World Economic Outlook (WEO), active Fund arrangement dates from the IMF Monitoring of Fund Arrangements (MONA) database, and country-specific commodity net export price series from the IMF Commodity Terms of Trade (CTOT) database (\citealt{gruss2019}). Systemic banking and currency crisis chronologies are taken from \citet{laeven2020}, and services, tourism, and remittance series are obtained from the World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI). Monthly economic activity and industrial production series are sourced from Haver Analytics under a commercial license and cannot be redistributed directly, but all series codes and transformations are documented in the replication package. The dynamic core-periphery model is simulated in Python (\texttt{scripts/model\_dynamic.py}), and the leave-one-out import-demand instrument, exposure-robust standard errors, and diagnostic checks in Appendix~\ref{app:robust} are implemented in \texttt{scripts/identification\_loo.py}. Replication code and public data extraction scripts are available from the author upon request.
