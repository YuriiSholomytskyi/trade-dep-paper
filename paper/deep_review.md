# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper investigates how bilateral external demand shapes post-crisis economic recovery across emerging and developing economies using a panel of 263 endogenously dated downturn episodes across 34 countries derived from high-frequency monthly activity indicators (2004–2024). By constructing a shift-share (Bartik) measure that links predetermined bilateral export shares to trading partners' subsequent growth—and complementing it with a leave-one-out destination import-demand design and theoretical core-periphery models—the author demonstrates that recovery speed is strongly accelerated by demand from established advanced-economy export hubs (predominantly the United States), whereas aggregate trade openness has no detectable stabilizing effect and rising trade links with China operate asymmetrically through upstream import supply rather than export absorption.

## Strengths
* The paper makes a compelling empirical contribution by combining high-frequency monthly activity indicators with partner-disaggregated shift-share econometrics to disentangle directional bilateral export pull from generic trade openness during economic downturns.
* The analysis uncovers a policy-relevant structural asymmetry in geoeconomic trade realignment, demonstrating that while emerging economies have increasingly re-coupled to China for upstream imports, the United States remains the primary counter-cyclical consumer of last resort for their exports.
* The theoretical synthesis neatly embeds an "import-light" expenditure composition tilt into both a static international portfolio risk-sharing framework and a dynamic core-periphery output-gap model, illustrating how structural shifts in core GDP growth (such as domestic AI services or reshoring) can weaken cross-border stabilization without reducing headline core growth.
* The empirical strategy is supported by an extensive battery of validation exercises, including leave-one-out destination import-demand shifters, permutation tests on exposure shares, discrete-time survival models accounting for right-censoring, and state-dependent local projections.

## Weaknesses
* The baseline finding of exclusive US-demand primacy in shortening recovery duration is sensitive to how recovery persistence and cyclical turning points are defined: when recovery requires the output gap to remain non-negative for three or six consecutive months (Appendix Table 14), the US duration coefficient loses statistical significance while other advanced economies become significant, suggesting the author might consider testing robustness against non-parametric turning-point algorithms or linear projection detrending that avoid Hodrick–Prescott filter artifacts (Reference Paper: Cyclical Analysis of Time Series: Selected Procedures and Computer Programs, Bry and Boschan, 1971; Reference Paper: Dissecting the Cycle: A Methodological Investigation, Harding and Pagan, 2002; Reference Paper: Why You Should Never Use the Hodrick-Prescott Filter, Hamilton, 2018).
* In the leave-one-out import-demand validation (Appendix Table 19), destination import growth is aggregated across all goods using annual IMF IMTS data rather than disaggregating at the product level and still overlaps temporally with the recovery window; the author might consider utilizing bilateral product-level trade data to isolate within-product demand innovations and reporting Adão–Kolesár–Morales (AKM) exposure-robust standard errors for the specification with destination and year fixed effects (Reference Paper: BACI: International Trade Database at the Product-Level, Gaulier and Zignago, 2010; Reference Paper: Shift-Share Designs: Theory and Inference, Adão, Kolesár, and Morales, 2019; Reference Paper: Quasi-Experimental Shift-Share Research Designs, Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel, 2022).
* Because the shift-share exposure weights rely exclusively on gross bilateral merchandise exports rather than domestic value-added absorbed by foreign final demand, the empirical specification may conflate intermediate supply-chain transshipment through connector economies with true final demand absorption, and the author might consider discussing or cross-checking against value-added trade measures (Reference Paper: Accounting for Intermediates: Production Sharing and Trade in Value Added, Johnson and Noguera, 2012; Reference Paper: Tracing Value-Added and Double Counting in Gross Exports, Koopman, Wang, and Wei, 2014).
* While modeling the import-light expenditure composition tilt in a core-periphery recovery setting is a valuable synthesis, the expenditure-composition parameters are calibrated illustratively rather than estimated from sectoral input-output data, and the author might consider contextualizing this mechanism within prior literature on import-intensity-adjusted aggregate demand and demand-composition shifts (Reference Paper: Estimating Trade Elasticities: Demand Composition and the Trade Collapse of 2008–2009, Bussière, Callegari, Ghironi, Sestieri, and Yamano, 2013; Reference Paper: Missing Imports in the Euro Area: Domestic Monetary Policy, Cross-Border Synchronization, and Demand Composition, Caselli and Dizioli, 2025).
* Several empirical tables across Section VI and Appendix D exhibit undocumented shifts in control sets and specification details—such as omitting partner volatility in the commodity depth regressions (Tables 4 and 16), omitting de facto financial openness across all regression tables despite its inclusion in Equation (8), and leaving regional hub regressions untabulated—which the author could standardize to improve transparency and reproducibility.

## Potential Issues And Suggestions
### [Introduction, Literature Review & Global Trade Connectedness] (Pages: 1-12)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Conflation of Nominal Dollar Trade Values and Real Trade Volumes in Table 1 (Section III, Page 11 of PDF / Page 10 of text):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In the text discussing Table 1, the series is introduced as *"An examination of trade volumes across income groups reinforces this conclusion. Indexing each economy's goods trade to its 2017–2019 average reveals that median trade reached 131 among advanced economies, 134 among emerging and developing economies, and 149 among low-income economies during 2023–2025 (Table 1)."* However, the table note indicates that the underlying data come from the IMF's monthly International Trade in Goods Statistics (IMTS), defined as total goods trade with the world (imports CIF plus exports FOB), and explicitly references the *"2022 commodity-price spike."* Standard monthly IMTS bilateral and world series are reported in current (nominal) US dollars rather than price-deflated real volumes. Because cumulative global dollar price inflation and commodity price shifts between 2017–2019 and 2023–2025 were substantial, an index of 131 to 149 in nominal dollar terms reflects both price-level increases and real volume expansion.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in the text and Table 1 caption whether the index is constructed from nominal US dollar trade values, and if so, refer to "nominal trade values" rather than "trade volumes" (or complement the table with price-deflated volume indices using WEO trade price deflators) so that dollar price inflation is not conflated with real volume growth.

2. **Gross Bilateral Trade Shares versus Indirect Supply-Chain (Value-Added) Exposure (Sections II–III, V, and VII–VIII, Pages 9–12, 16–18, and 29–31 of PDF / Pages 8–11, 15–17, and 29–31 of text):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section III and Figures 1–2 document a major reorganization of global supply chains between 2021 and 2026: the United States halved its direct import share from China while sharply increasing imports from connector economies such as Vietnam and Mexico, even as the rest of the world increased the share of its imports sourced from China. While this descriptive evidence motivates the contrast between upstream supply hubs and final demand anchors—as well as the policy and surveillance implications in Sections VII and VIII regarding the vulnerability of manufacturing exporters in the Americas to a potential "import-light" shift in US growth—both the descriptive statistics in Section III and the subsequent shift-share weights ($s_{ij,\text{pre}}$) in Section V rely exclusively on *gross* bilateral merchandise trade from IMTS. When supply chains are rerouted through third-country assembly hubs, gross bilateral trade flows conflate intermediate input trade with final demand absorption. Specifically, an emerging economy exporting intermediates to China or to a regional manufacturing hub for final assembly and sale to the United States has indirect exposure to US final demand that gross bilateral export shares assign to the immediate shipping partner rather than the final consumer; conversely, a connector economy's gross exports to the United States overstate its domestic value-added exposure if a rising fraction of those exports embodies imported Chinese inputs. Furthermore, gross export shares cannot directly distinguish exports absorbed by Chinese domestic final demand from inputs used in China's outward processing trade.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Discuss the distinction between gross bilateral trade linkages and trade in value added (TiVA) in Sections II–III, Section V, and Sections VII–VIII (or cross-check against value-added export shares such as OECD TiVA where available). Explicitly explaining how indirect supply-chain linkages affect the interpretation of China's role as an upstream supplier versus the United States as a final consumer—and noting how multi-country supply-chain rerouting maps into gross bilateral export shares—would strengthen the conceptual bridge between Section III, the empirical strategy, and the policy conclusions.

3. **Distinction Between Output Shares in the Theoretical Model and Export Shares in the Empirical Specification (Abstract, Section I, and Section IV.A, Pages 2, 7, and 12 of PDF / Pages 1, 6, and 11 of text):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Equation (1) on page 12 of the PDF (page 11 of the text), $y_i = (1 - s_i)\varepsilon_i + s_i \mu$, the weight $s_i \in [0, 1]$ is defined as *"the share of country $i$'s output absorbed by the core"* (i.e., bilateral exports to the core relative to domestic output or GDP, $X_{i,\text{core}}/Y_i$). In contrast, the empirical shift-share measure introduced in the Abstract and Introduction weights partner growth by country $i$'s bilateral share of *total exports* ($X_{ij}/X_i$), which sums to unity across destinations regardless of the ratio of total exports to GDP ($X_i/Y_i$), while aggregate trade openness is entered as a separate additive control. Under Equation (1), two economies with the same US share of total exports ($X_{i,\text{US}}/X_i$) would experience different output stabilization depending on their overall export-to-GDP intensity ($X_i/Y_i$).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify the mapping between $s_i$ in Equation (1) (exports to the core as a share of total output) and the empirical exposure weights (exports to the core as a share of total exports), or briefly explain how scaling bilateral export shares by export-to-GDP intensity relates to the additive specification.

4. **Visual and Temporal Alignment of China's Export Share Between Global Trends and the Estimation Sample (Sections I & III, Pages 7 & 10–11 of PDF / Pages 6 & 9–10 of text):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section III motivates China's limited role as a demand anchor using global bilateral trade changes between 2021 and early 2026, noting that while China's share of national imports rose across 74 percent of countries, *"national export shares directed to China remained virtually flat over the same period."* However, Figure 1 only plots import shares from China without displaying the corresponding distribution or trajectory of export shares directed to China. In addition, as later noted in Footnote 4 (page 22 of PDF / page 21 of text), major South American commodity exporters in the 34-country estimation sample (such as Chile and Peru, for which China became the primary export destination in the 2010s) only contribute downturn episodes from 2004–2013 and are therefore classified into the European Union hub based on earlier pre-downturn trade shares.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Consider reporting the corresponding export-share summary statistics or panel alongside Figure 1 in Section III to visually substantiate the asymmetry between import and export shares with China, and briefly note in Section I or III that the minimal export exposure to China in the regression sample reflects both structural trade asymmetry and the historical pre-downturn timing of the sample episodes.

### [Theoretical Portfolio & Dynamic Core-Periphery Models] (Pages: 12-16, 46-50)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Autoregressive Specification of the Import-Intensive Shock and Initial Peripheral Conditions in the Dynamic Model (Section IV.B, pp. 14–15; Appendix E, pp. 45–49)**
   - **Law of motion for $\varepsilon_t^c$ in Equation (11):** In Appendix E, Equation (10) explicitly specifies an $\text{AR}(1)$ process for import-light activity ($\hat{q}_t = \rho_q \hat{q}_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t^q$, with $0 < \rho_q < 1$), whereas Equation (11) introduces the import-intensive absorption disturbance $\varepsilon_t^c$ directly into the forward-looking Euler equation,
     $$\hat{c}_t = \mathbb{E}_t \hat{c}_{t+1} - \sigma \hat{r}_t - \chi \hat{q}_t + \varepsilon_t^c,$$
     without stating a dynamic law of motion for $\varepsilon_t^c$, and Table 21 lists only a single core persistence parameter ($\rho_q = 0.70$). This is a potential concern for analytical completeness and reproducibility because if $\varepsilon_t^c$ is treated as an i.i.d. innovation when $\hat{q}_t = 0$, the forward-looking absorption equation $(1 + \sigma \phi_y \omega_c)\hat{c}_t = \mathbb{E}_t \hat{c}_{t+1} + \varepsilon_t^c$ has no endogenous persistence: $\hat{c}_t$ and $\hat{y}_t^C$ would jump on impact at $t = 0$ and return immediately to zero for $t \ge 1$. Reproducing the identical core output trajectories across horizons in the left panel of Figure 4 (p. 15) and the calibrated impact and cumulative import responses reported on page 48 (where normalizing $\Delta \hat{y}_0^C = 1$ yields $\hat{m}_0 = \frac{\eta_c}{\omega_c} = \frac{0.60}{0.55} \approx 1.09 \approx 1.1$ on impact and $\frac{1.0909}{1 - 0.70} \approx 3.64 \approx 3.6$ cumulated for the import-intensive shock, versus $\hat{m}_0 \approx 0.23 \approx 0.2$ on impact and $\approx 0.78 \approx 0.8$ cumulated for the import-light shock) requires that the import-intensive demand shifter $\varepsilon_t^c$ also follow an $\text{AR}(1)$ process with persistence $\rho_c = \rho_q = 0.70$, as noted qualitatively in the prose on page 48 ("each core expansion is a persistent ($\rho = 0.7$) push"). Explicitly writing out the $\text{AR}(1)$ law of motion for $\varepsilon_t^c$ alongside Equation (11) would make the analytical system self-contained.
   - **Peripheral initial shock and Figure 4 right-panel details:** While Table 21 provides the parameters for the core block and peripheral persistence, it omits the magnitude of the initial peripheral domestic shock $\varepsilon_{i,0}^P$ (or initial output gap $\hat{y}_{i,0}^P$, which appears to be roughly $-0.9$ in the right panel of Figure 4) and the scale of the normalized core output shock ($\Delta \hat{y}_0^C = 1$) needed to reproduce the peripheral recovery durations of about three quarters at $\gamma_i = 0.35$ against eleven quarters at $\gamma_i = 0.10$ (pp. 48–49). In addition, clarifying directly in the Figure 4 caption (p. 15) that the right panel plots peripheral recovery under the import-intensive expansion (and optionally contrasting it with the recovery duration under the import-light expansion analyzed in Proposition 1) would connect the figure more directly to the proposition.

2. **Discounted vs. Undiscounted Cumulative Import-Demand Condition in Proposition 1 (Appendix E, p. 47)**
   - In the formal statement of Proposition 1, the condition for $\hat{y}_{i,t}^{P,c} \ge \hat{y}_{i,t}^{P,q}$ is stated as holding "for every horizon at which the cumulative import-demand difference is nonnegative" ($\sum_{j=0}^t \Delta \hat{m}_j \ge 0$). However, in the proof immediately below, iterating Equation (16) forward yields:
     $$\Delta \hat{y}_{i,t}^P = \alpha \gamma_i \sum_{j=0}^t \rho_P^{t-j} \Delta \hat{m}_j,$$
     which the proof and the subsequent discussion on page 48 accurately describe as a "nonnegative discounted cumulative import-demand difference."
   - This presents a potential mathematical discrepancy between the wording in the proposition statement and its proof. Because peripheral output persistence satisfies $0 < \rho_P < 1$, the weight $\rho_P^{t-j}$ acts as a backward-decaying memory kernel that assigns greater weight to more recent import differences ($j = t$) and exponentially smaller weight to initial differences ($j = 0$). If $\Delta \hat{m}_j \ge 0$ holds pointwise for all $0 \le j \le t$ (which holds automatically when both shocks share the same $\text{AR}(1)$ persistence), both the unweighted and $\rho_P$-weighted sums are nonnegative at every horizon. However, for general shock processes where $\Delta \hat{m}_j$ could change sign over time (for example, if the two core shocks had different persistence parameters such that $\Delta \hat{m}_j$ were positive initially and negative at later horizons), an unweighted cumulative import-demand difference $\sum_{j=0}^t \Delta \hat{m}_j \ge 0$ is not sufficient to guarantee $\sum_{j=0}^t \rho_P^{t-j} \Delta \hat{m}_j \ge 0$. Stating the condition in Proposition 1 explicitly in terms of the $\rho_P$-discounted cumulative import-demand difference $\sum_{j=0}^t \rho_P^{t-j} \Delta \hat{m}_j \ge 0$ (or pointwise dominance $\Delta \hat{m}_j \ge 0$ for all $0 \le j \le t$) would ensure exact consistency between the proposition and its proof.

3. **Domain Condition for $s_i(\theta) < s^\star$ and Numerical Illustration in Corollary 1 (Section IV.A, pp. 11–13)**
   - **Conditioning domain for $s_i(\theta) < s^\star$:** Corollary 1 states: *"As the core economy allocates a larger share of absorption toward import-light activity (so that $\theta$ rises and the effective export share $s_i(\theta)$ falls below $s^\star$), periphery output variance increases: $\frac{\partial \text{Var}(y_i)}{\partial \theta} = -\omega_i \frac{\partial \text{Var}(y_i)}{\partial s_i} > 0$."* Because $\text{Var}(y_i) = (1 - s_i)^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + s_i^2 \sigma_\mu^2$ is strictly convex with a unique minimum at $s^\star = \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \sigma_\mu^2}$, the marginal derivative $\frac{\partial \text{Var}(y_i)}{\partial \theta}$ is strictly positive if and only if $\omega_i > 0$ and the effective export share lies in the sub-optimal region $s_i(\theta) = \omega_i(1-\theta) < s^\star$. While this condition holds across the empirical range of export shares and throughout the numerical calibration (where $\omega_i \le 0.55 < s^\star = \frac{25}{29} \approx 0.862$), if an economy started with $\omega_i > s^\star$ and $\theta$ rose so that $s_i(\theta)$ crossed below $s^\star$, output variance would initially *decrease* while $s_i(\theta) \in (s^\star, \omega_i]$ and only increase once $s_i(\theta) < s^\star$. Rephrasing Corollary 1 to state $\omega_i > 0$ and $s_i(\theta) < s^\star$ explicitly as conditioning assumptions—rather than phrasing $s_i(\theta)$ falling below $s^\star$ as a parenthetical consequence of $\theta$ rising—would make the formal statement exact.
   - **Numerical example on page 12:** Under the baseline parameters $\sigma_\varepsilon = 5$ and $\sigma_\mu = 2$ (which yield an $86.2\%$ variance reduction at $s^\star \approx 0.862$ relative to autarky), explicitly noting in the text that the "approximately 16 percent" average variance increase as $\theta$ rises from $0.2$ to $0.5$ is calculated across the three structural exposure regimes plotted in Figure 3 ($\omega_i \in \{0.10, 0.30, 0.55\}$) would help readers directly verify the calculation.

The fourth numerical column is labeled `GDP-growth ratio` (reporting values of $2.57$ for the United States, $1.25$ for the European Union, $3.77$ for China, and $1.01$ for Japan), and the caption states that *"Ranking hubs by GDP growth instead (last column) misleads, placing China first,"* without explicitly defining the formula for `GDP-growth ratio`.

### [Data, Episode Dating & Shift-Share Identification Strategy] (Pages: 16-19, 35-38)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Missing Implementation Details and Potential Mechanical Properties in HP-Filter Episode Dating (Section V, pp. 15–16; Appendix A, Table 8, p. 34; Appendix C, Tables 10–11, pp. 35–36)**
   - **Missing pre-processing and parameter details (Reproducibility):** Section V ("Episode dating and duration metrics," p. 16) states that downturn episodes, trough depths, and recovery durations are constructed using the Hodrick–Prescott (HP) output gap from seasonally adjusted monthly central-bank economic activity indices and industrial production series. However, several specifications required to reproduce the 263 episodes and their summary statistics in Table 10 are omitted:
     1. *Smoothing parameter, transformation, and series prioritization:* Neither Section V nor Appendix A specifies the monthly HP smoothing parameter $\lambda$ (e.g., $\lambda = 14{,}400$ vs. $\lambda = 129{,}600$), whether the monthly indices are log-transformed prior to filtering (or whether the output gap is computed as a percentage deviation from the HP trend), or how central-bank economic activity indices and industrial production series are prioritized when both are available for a country. Because industrial production is typically more volatile than broad GDP-proxy activity indices, applying a uniform $2\%$ output-gap trough threshold across both series types is a potential concern that could affect cross-country episode counts and measured depths.
     2. *Initialization of the one-sided filter and censoring/boundary conventions:* The minimum burn-in window required to initialize the expanding real-time HP filter is not stated. Furthermore, in Table 10 (p. 35), the mean *Contraction* is $3.3$ months (with a minimum of $0.0$ months) and the mean *Climb* is $6.8$ months ($3.3 + 6.8 = 10.1$), whereas the mean *Full negative cycle* is $9.9$ months, and both *Climb* and *Full negative cycle* are top-coded at $60.0$ months. Explicitly stating the month-counting convention for peak, trough, and zero-crossing (and how the 60-month right-censoring cap is applied to *Climb* versus *Full negative cycle*) would explain why the sum of the component means exceeds the full-cycle mean and how a contraction of $0.0$ months arises.
   - **Potential methodological concern regarding two-sided HP filtering and unconstrained phase durations:** Under the baseline two-sided HP filter, the estimated trend at month $t$ incorporates post-trough future observations. Consequently, a rapid post-crisis rebound pulls the estimated contemporaneous trend upward during the downturn spell itself, while a prolonged slump pulls the trend downward to meet stagnant output, potentially creating a zero-crossing ("recovery" to trend) even without output rebounding toward its pre-crisis trajectory. Moreover, because episodes are dated without minimum phase or cycle duration constraints (such as the standard 5-month minimum phase and 15-month minimum cycle in the monthly Bry–Boschan / Harding–Pagan algorithm), high-frequency volatility or brief crossings of the HP trend can split single macro slumps into multiple short spells. This appears to contribute to episodes as short as $1.0$ month in Table 10 and substantial cross-country imbalance in Table 11 (p. 36), where Costa Rica accounts for 29 distinct episodes over a 21-year span (more than one downturn per year) while economies such as Chile, Mexico, and Ukraine have only 1 to 3 episodes. While the paper checks robustness using a one-sided HP filter and 3- and 6-month persistent returns, complementing the HP-gap dating with a non-parametric turning-point algorithm (e.g., Bry–Boschan) or the Hamilton (2018) linear projection filter—or imposing a minimum phase/cycle duration rule—would help verify that episode durations and counts are not driven by HP trend adjustment.

2. **Ambiguity in Time Windows, Share Normalization, and Scaling of the Shift-Share Measures (Section V, Equations 7–9, pp. 16–18; Appendix A, Table 8, p. 34; Appendix C, Tables 10–11, pp. 35–36)**
   - **Annual-average vs. cumulative growth scaling in the magnitude interpretation:** Equation (7) (p. 16) and Table 8 (p. 34) define external demand as the export-share-weighted average of trading partners' real GDP growth over a three-year window spanning the peak year and the two subsequent years ($\bar{g}_{j,\text{window}}$). In Table 10 (p. 35), `External demand (shift-share, %)` has a mean and median of `2.6%`, indicating that $\bar{g}_{j,\text{window}}$ is the *annualized average* real GDP growth rate over $[e, e+2]$ rather than cumulative three-year growth. However, when illustrating the magnitude of a 1 percentage-point increase in the US demand contribution ($s_{i,\text{US},\text{pre}} \cdot \bar{g}_{\text{US},\text{window}}$) on p. 18, the text states: *"For an economy directing 10 percent of its exports to the United States, this corresponds to an additional 10 percentage points of US real GDP growth over the window."* Under an annualized three-year average, a 10 percentage-point increase in $\bar{g}_{\text{US},\text{window}}$ corresponds to an extra 10 percentage points of US real GDP growth *per year* for three years (roughly 30 percentage points cumulative), which is well outside historical US variation. Explicitly stating the formula for $\bar{g}_{j,\text{window}}$ in Equation (7) and reconciling the p. 18 illustration with the annual-average scaling in Table 10 would resolve this ambiguity.
   - **Timing and normalization of predetermined export shares ($s_{ij,\text{pre}}$ vs. $s_{ij,e-1}$):** Equation (7) (p. 16) weights partner growth by $s_{ij,\text{pre}}$ ("measured before the downturn"), whereas Equation (9) (p. 17) indexes the pre-downturn share by calendar year $s_{ij,e-1}$ for an episode with peak year $e$. Because episodes are dated at a monthly frequency and trade shares are calculated over trailing 12-month windows from monthly IMTS data, it is unclear whether $s_{ij,\text{pre}}$ in Equation (7) is computed over the trailing 12 months ending immediately prior to the peak *month* of the episode or over the calendar year $e-1$ prior to the peak *year*, and whether the shares across the four mutually exclusive partner groups sum strictly to unity ($\sum_j s_{ij,\text{pre}} = 1$, normalized by total world exports) or leave an unallocated residual. Similarly, in Table 11 (p. 36), each country is assigned to a single regional hub across all of its episodes; clarifying whether hub assignment in Table 11 is defined on period-average shares or happens not to switch across episodes for any country in the sample would aid replication.
   - **Time horizon of the leave-one-out shift ($g_{j,e}^{-i}$):** In Equation (9) (p. 17), the leave-one-out destination import growth rate is written with a single peak-year subscript $g_{j,e}^{-i}$, whereas the baseline shift $\bar{g}_{j,\text{window}}$ in Equation (7) averages growth over $[e, e+2]$. Because the text on p. 18 notes that *"the import-growth window still overlaps part of the recovery,"* it should be clarified in Section V whether $g_{j,e}^{-i}$ is measured only in calendar year $e$ or averaged over the multi-year window $[e, e+2]$, and how the Adão et al. (2019) exposure-robust standard errors aggregate across destination-by-year $(j,e)$ shock cells when countries experience multiple episodes.

2. **Methodological Specification and Inference in the Leave-One-Out (LOO) Import-Demand Analysis (Appendix D, Table 19, pp. 42–43)**
   The leave-one-out shift-share analysis in Appendix D provides an important validation check, but four technical and reporting details present potential concerns:
   - *Additive vs. Interacted Fixed Effects in Column (3):* In Table 19 (p. 43), the row label for Column (3) reads `Destination×year FE in shift: Yes`, whereas the text (p. 42) and table caption (p. 43) state that the shifts are estimated *"after removing destination and year fixed effects"* (*"destination and year residualization"*). This is a critical distinction for reproducibility: because the underlying annual trade data carry no product dimension, destination import growth $g_{j,e}^{-i}$ varies only at the destination-by-year ($j \times e$) level up to the subtraction of economy $i$'s own exports. Residualizing against interacted destination-by-year fixed effects ($\alpha_{j,e}$) would absorb 100% of the variation in destination $j$'s aggregate import growth in year $e$, leaving only the mechanical variation from subtracting economy $i$'s own exports. If additive fixed effects ($\alpha_j + \tau_e$) were used, the row label in Table 19 should be corrected to `Destination and year FE in shift`. In addition, moving from the raw LOO shift in Column (2) to the residualized shift in Column (3) causes the point estimate to more than triple from $-0.27^{**}$ (SE $0.10$) to $-0.91^{***}$ (SE $0.24$); briefly explaining whether this shift reflects reduced variance/rescaling of the residualized shifter or confounding absorbed by the fixed effects would be helpful.
   - *Exposure-Robust (AKM) Standard Errors with Few Effective Shocks (Column 4):* In Column (4) of Table 19, applying Adão–Kolesár–Morales (AKM) exposure-robust standard errors to the *raw* LOO specification of Column (2) reduces the standard error by $60\%$ (from $0.10$ to $0.04$, increasing $|t|$ from $2.70$ to $6.75$). Under the shift-share frameworks of Adão et al. (2019) and Borusyak et al. (2022), valid shock-level inference in panel data generally requires isolating shock innovations orthogonal to common period (year) fixed effects so that global cycles do not induce cross-destination correlation, and relies asymptotically on a large number of effective independent shocks with small maximum exposure weights. As noted on page 43, the median inverse-Herfindahl index here is only about six destinations and the median largest exposure weight is $0.33$. In finite samples with high concentration in a dominant hub (the United States across roughly 20 peak years), asymptotic AKM standard errors can suffer from substantial downward bias. Reporting exposure-robust standard errors for the residualized specification in Column (3) and explicitly noting the finite-sample caveat of few effective destination shocks would strengthen the methodological discussion.
   - *Unreported Partner-Type Decomposition and Diagnostics:* On pages 42–43, the text states that decomposing the LOO import-demand measure by partner type reproduces the baseline pattern—citing a significant US full-cycle coefficient of $-0.88$ ($t = -3.0$) while the other-advanced, emerging, and China terms are individually insignificant—and cites balance regressions and a pre-recovery contraction placebo. However, Table 19 only tabulates the aggregate `External pull` and does not specify whether the $-0.88$ ($t = -3.0$) estimate comes from the raw LOO specification or the residualized specification. Adding a second panel to Table 19 with the partner-disaggregated LOO estimates would substantiate this claim.
   - *Conceptual Tension in the Contraction-Duration Placebo:* On page 43, the text presents a regression of peak-to-trough contraction duration on the LOO shift as a *"pre-recovery placebo"* and interprets the statistically insignificant loading ($t = -1.5$) as evidence against reverse causality. However, in the main text (Table 3, Column 2, p. 20, and Section VI.A, p. 23), contraction duration is treated as a *valid treatment outcome* where a significant negative US coefficient ($-2.23^{***}$, $t = -3.1$ in Table 3; $-2.1$, $t = -3.4$ on p. 23) is cited as confirming the demand stabilization mechanism with minimal recovery-window overlap. Reconciling why contraction duration is interpreted as a substantive treatment outcome in Section VI but as a null placebo test in Appendix D would resolve an apparent conceptual contradiction.

5. **Right-Censoring Non-Additivity, Endogenous Depth Control, and Survival Risk-Set Timing (Table 3, p. 20; Table 16, p. 40; Table 20, p. 44)**
   - *Non-Additivity of Linear Duration Coefficients in Tables 3 and 16:* By definition (Table 3 note, p. 20), $\text{Full cycle} = \text{Contraction} + \text{Climb}$. In an uncensored OLS regression with identical covariates, the coefficients on `Contraction` and `Climb` must sum identically to the `Full cycle` coefficient ($\hat{\beta}_{\text{Contraction}} + \hat{\beta}_{\text{Climb}} = \hat{\beta}_{\text{Full cycle}}$). In Table 3 (p. 20) and Table 16 (p. 40), they differ slightly (e.g., in Table 3, for `US demand`, $-2.23 + (-3.60) = -5.83 \neq -5.59$; for `China demand`, $-0.21 + 2.57 = 2.36 \neq 1.86$). This discrepancy arises because both `Climb` and `Full cycle` are independently top-coded (right-censored) at $60$ months for the $8$ episodes that do not return to trend within the tracking window (as reflected in Table 10, p. 36), so $\min(\text{Contraction} + \text{Climb}, 60) \neq \text{Contraction} + \min(\text{Climb}, 60)$. Briefly noting this independent $60$-month top-coding in the caption of Table 3 would prevent readers from interpreting the discrepancy as a calculation error.
   - *Conditioning Pre-Trough Durations on `Depth of downturn` (Tables 3 and 20):* Both the `Contraction` regression in Table 3 (Column 2, p. 20) and the discrete-time proportional-hazards model in Table 20 (p. 44, where `Person-months at risk = 2609` corresponds to the full cycle measured from the initial peak, $263 \times 9.9 \approx 2604$ months) include `Depth of downturn` (`Shock depth`) as a time-invariant covariate. Because an episode cannot return to trend before reaching its trough and shock depth is only realized at the trough (the end of the contraction phase), controlling for terminal depth when modeling the contraction duration or the hazard of recovery starting from the initial peak conditions pre-trough months on a future outcome. Clarifying that the hazard model in Table 20 and the contraction regression in Table 3 are robust to omitting `Shock depth` (or starting the survival clock at the trough to model the `Climb` phase) would address this look-ahead conditioning concern.
